The night

Five things happen. None of them need you.

You start the show and play. Everything below runs itself while you're on stage.

1

They scan, and they're in

One code on the table. No app, no sign-up, no email, no password — the fans never make an account, which is exactly why it works in a bar at 10pm on patchy wifi.

Print it once. It's good forever.

2

You stop guessing

One screen tells you what's winning, how many people are in the room, and what they've asked for. One tap starts the top song and hands everybody fresh votes.

No more standing in the gap doing maths.

3

They ask for things — properly

A song you haven't got listed. A happy birthday for Maya at table four. Requests cost votes, never money, so nobody's buying their way onto your setlist — and they only exist if you switch them on.

Say no and their votes come straight back, automatically. You never have to be the bad guy.

4

Money happens without you asking

Out of votes and desperate for one more song? They can buy a few. Had a great night? There's a tip button with your name on it and a note you'll see on stage.

You never hold out a jar. You never interrupt a song to mention Venmo. It's just there, at the exact moment they want to use it.

5

And afterwards, they can find you

Everyone who voted was on a page with your name at the top. There's a Lyrics button on it too, so the room can sing along — free on every plan, forever, because a sing-along that works at one gig and not the next is worse than none at all.

Your page holds your music, your links and every gig you've got coming. And your gigs land in a public what's-on feed for your town, so people looking for something to do tonight find you.

Straight answers

The things you're actually wondering.

Do my fans have to download anything?
No — and they never will. They scan a code, a web page opens, they vote. No app, no account, no email, no password. That is the single most important design decision in MySet, because anything more than "scan and tap" doesn't happen in a bar.
What if nobody votes?
Then you play what you were going to play anyway and nothing is worse than before. In practice one table starts, the rest see them doing it, and it spreads. On the first real gig it ran, eight people voted twenty-one times in a room that had no idea it existed an hour earlier.
What if they request something I can't play?
Requests are off until you turn them on, and they cost votes rather than money. When one comes in you can add it to your setlist, mark it done, or decline — and declining refunds them automatically, so you're never the villain and nobody's out of pocket.
Will it work on the venue's terrible wifi?
Everyone's on their own phone and their own data, so you're not sharing one connection. The page is tiny, it backs off politely when the room goes quiet, and if a phone drops out it picks up where it left off. Your own screen is the only one that matters, and it's one page.
I play originals. Is this only for covers?
It's your setlist — originals, covers, whatever you've got. Voting on originals is arguably better: you find out which of your own songs a room full of strangers actually reaches for.
Can people see my whole setlist? Is that a problem?
They see what you choose to show. Any song can be hidden, and it stays hidden across every future show until you bring it back. For one night only, build a setlist instead — name it, put the songs you want in it, and the room sees just those. You can keep twenty of them and attach one to a gig in your calendar, so it loads itself when you start the show.
How long does setting up actually take?
About two minutes to a working page: email, a six-digit code, your name. Adding songs takes as long as typing them, and there's a starter pack of around sixty well-known covers you can add in one tap and edit down.
