The finding

A hundred times the people is ten thousand times the work

Every time a phone checks in, the server re-reads the entire audience — everyone in the room, merged from twelve shards. So each check-in gets more expensive as the room fills, and there are more of them. The two multiply.

That is the whole problem, and it is invisible in the cost model. loadsim.py prices a poll at 2,530 bytes on the wire, which is true — but the bytes the server moves to answer it grow with every person standing in the room. Folding 10,000 fans costs only 5.7ms of CPU. It is not the arithmetic. It is the reading.

What four systems said

Two of them overturned the thing I had already built

Eleven real systems studied, four architectures designed, and four load-bearing claims handed to adversarial fact-checkers whose only job was to refute them. All four claims came back wrong — including two of mine.

The first version of this work was a hard door: person 501 at a Plus gig becomes a read-only watcher. That is now deleted, and there are tests whose only job is to stop it coming back.

Mentimeter We will never prevent participants from joining and voting on an ongoing presentation.

Killed the door. You may exceed the limit mid-session with no interruption, with an eight-hour grace period. The cap bites on the next show — published policy at the closest comparable product on the market.

YouTube Live chat The client sleeps exactly the timeoutMs the server hands back.

Became the fix. At streams past eight million concurrent viewers, the interval is stamped on every single response. MySet’s ladder lived in the page, so the only way to slow a room was a deploy every phone had to reload.

Discord ~90% of connections in large servers are passive — making fanout 90% less expensive.

Named the squared term. MySet’s rule was the exact inverse: any one vote reset every phone in the room to the fastest rung. One person’s tap became maximum work from everybody.

Uber At some point, 80% of requests made to the backend API gateway were polling calls.

Set the direction. Their fix was not a faster poll or a cheaper database — it was letting the backend decide when the app gets the next thing. And a warning: their scale transfers badly, because their problems are independent and MySet’s is one shared object.

Polling is not the embarrassing shortcut here. It is what the biggest live-chat system on earth does.

Two of these were my own working assumptions, and one of them — the cache header — was the fix recommended in the first version of this page. It would have been a day spent for nothing.

Shipped

The server sets the pace, and the room slows itself down

An ordinary pub gig is untouched — the same three seconds it has always been. A packed room quietly widens its own interval and shows the top of the board instead of all of it, and says so on screen.

Then the research found the single line that mattered most, and it was already in the file. signature() included the running tally, so any vote by anyone reset every phone in the room to the fastest rung. In a busy room somebody is always voting — so the 10-second and 25-second rungs were unreachable, and what looked like a ladder was a fixed 3-second poll wearing a ladder’s clothes.

There are two signatures now. Below the size where the server widens the interval, nothing changes at all: at eight people the tally jumping the instant somebody votes is the product, and it costs a fraction of a cent. Above it, only the artist’s actions and your own credits wake the room; the board still redraws, it just stops accelerating everybody. That last part is not what the research recommended — it recommended dropping crowd-acceleration everywhere — and the difference is deliberate.

Two more things went in the same commit, both cheap and both impossible to retrofit mid-show: ±20% jitter on every scheduled poll, because the server hands every phone the same interval and without it they all fire on the same tick; and a terminal rung, so a phone left face-up with nobody looking at it settles at a minute instead of retrying for ever.

It came down to 2,000. Then the ladder fix landed and 2,000 turned out to sit at 30 MB/s, so the number is now conservative rather than marginal. It stays at 2,000 until the shared board makes a bigger one honest.
The pricing answer

Don’t switch to pay-per-gig. You already have it, and yours is better

The 10% / 2% / 0% cut is usage-based pricing. It charges when the artist earns rather than when they play — the right trigger for lumpy income, and a kinder meter than per-gig. A musician who plays twenty quiet nights pays nothing extra.

Per-gig would also meter the wrong thing. A normal gig costs between three and sixteen cents. Charging by the gig meters pennies while leaving the genuinely unbounded quantity — the size of the room — free.

1

Sell the slot, not the seat

Free to vote; pay to dedicate a song, name the request, or push it up. You already own the scarce surface — the top of the setlist.

At 10,000 people the night costs $36.40 and no $20/month plan covers it. A $5 floor on a visible slot does — and paid actions throttle the write path by their nature. This is Super Chat’s shape, and it is the strongest idea in the research.

2

A one-off big-night pass, off the ladder

Not a fourth tier. An arena night is an event, not a subscription level — so it gets priced as one, booked in advance for that date. $99 up to 5,000, $249 up to 15,000.

Slido charges $250 for one 1,000-person event and $700 for 5,000; Mentimeter’s conference passes are $350 and $750. At $99 for 5,000 you are seven times under Slido and still roughly ninety times your own cost. And the real thing being sold is the notice — you find out the arena show is coming before it starts.

3

A human gate above the top tier

Above the Pro room, ask for two days’ notice. Publish it as a rule rather than discovering it during a set.

Mentimeter requires an email at least two business days ahead of any presentation over 10,000 participants. It turns an unbounded engineering risk into a scheduled, chargeable, prepared event — the cheapest item on this list and the one best suited to one person.

On “four free gigs, then pay per gig”: it puts friction exactly where someone is discovering that MySet works. The transaction cut already handles that transition, and handles it without anybody having to think about money before walking on stage.

The road

Four stages, and the signal that says build the next one

The 100,000 answer is not on this list as work. Nobody in this market serves it: Kahoot’s shipping client tops out at a 5,000 flag, Figma caps a live file at 500 editors, and Epic could not get 200 players onto one server. The answer to the arena is a contact form and a date.

The 10,000 stage is the shared board: split the poll into one response that is byte-identical for every phone (no fan= in the URL, so it can genuinely cache) and a tiny personal one called about eight times a gig instead of eight hundred. Blob reads fall from 13 million a gig to roughly 300,000, and the curve stops being quadratic. The 100,000 stage is Durable Objects with hibernating WebSockets — and a relay tier above ~8,000 phones, because one object is capped at 1,000 requests a second.

Still open

What would change the answer

Three of these are measurements nobody has taken, and each is an hour or less. They are worth taking before the next stage, not during it.

One hour · changes the plan

Does Netlify bill a cache hit?

Their docs count “content hosted on your project” with no exclusion for cached responses, so the pessimistic reading was used throughout. If hits are free, the shared board’s floor drops from about $2.20 a gig to $0.30 and the case for ever leaving Netlify mostly disappears. Deploy a cached function, hit it ten thousand times, read the meter tomorrow.

Two curl calls · day two

Does the durable cache survive the /api rewrite?

An existing invariant already records that Netlify-Vary is silently ignored on that path — measured, not theorised. If durable is ignored too, the board must be served from the function URL directly, which changes the shape and one line of the security policy. Recoverable on day two, painful on day five.

Never measured

How fast can one shard actually be written?

The write ceiling of ~24 writes a second per shard is derived from a measured 40 ms read, not from a measured write. If the real figure is a quarter of that, the write wall arrives at 4,000 people rather than 11,000. Write the script that hammers one shard before selling any room over 2,000.

Deliberately not built

Websockets on Netlify

A held connection bills as compute rather than as requests — the wrong side of the price sheet. And one publish to N subscribers bills as N+1 messages on Ably: roughly $270 an event at 10,000 people. Websockets are the right answer only on Cloudflare, where outbound messages are free.
