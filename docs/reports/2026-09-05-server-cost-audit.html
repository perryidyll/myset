The question

If MySet goes viral, does the server bill eat the business?

No. A three‑hour bar set with twenty people voting costs two point eight cents to serve. At ten thousand artists paying $10 a month, audience traffic is about 5% of revenue.

What is currently over budget is shipping code: 104 deploys last month cost 372 gigs’ worth of traffic. Everything below is measured — the payload with curl against the live site, the blob reads by the test suite, the deploys from Netlify’s own API. The polling behaviour is simulated because it has to be; the simulator is in the repo and runs the same ladder the app runs.

MECHANISM

What actually creates a request

Nothing in MySet pushes. There is no websocket and no server‑sent stream — every phone in the room asks, and each ask is one billable request plus a slice of function time. So the whole cost question reduces to a single number: how often does a phone ask?

The obvious answer is wrong by more than a factor of ten. The voting page does not poll on a fixed interval. It walks a ladder, and it stops entirely when the screen goes off:

The polling ladder, as public/vote.html runs it

every 3s For the first two ticks after anything changes — a vote from anyone in the room, a song change, the window opening. This is when people are actually watching.
every 10s Four ticks with nothing new. Still comfortably live.
every 25s A quiet room, between songs. Pulling down refreshes instantly, so nobody is stuck waiting for it.
never Screen off, or another app in front: no request at all. The timer keeps running; the fetch does not happen. Turning the screen back on drops straight to 3 seconds.

A scroll or a tap only lifts a phone out of the deepest rung — it does not pin it to 3 seconds. That one distinction was worth about two thirds of all polls: before it, 66% were held on the fast rung by fingers rather than by anything actually changing.

Simulated over a three‑hour set with twenty phones and a plausible amount of looking at them — about a fifth of the gig, in glances — the ladder produces 6,030 polls. That is 302 per phone: one every 36 seconds of wall clock. The naive figure, twenty phones every three seconds for three hours, is 72,000. The ladder is a 12× reduction, and nobody in the room can perceive it.

THE METER

How Netlify charges now

Netlify moved to credits. There is no free bucket of invocations any more and no per‑GB bandwidth price — everything draws on one pool, and you buy more when it runs out. Your account is on Personal: 1,000 credits for $9, top‑ups at 500 for $5.

Compute is nearly two thirds of the bill, and compute is not the code. The poll spends its 155 milliseconds mostly waiting: it does fifteen reads from Netlify Blobs, in parallel, at roughly 40 ms each. That number is not an estimate — the test suite counts it on every run and fails the build if it goes above fifteen.

Bandwidth is almost nothing today because the payload is small and photos are cached at the edge. That changes the moment video takes off, which is the one line worth watching.

The finding

Shipping code costs 29× more than serving the audience. One deploy is 15 credits; a whole three‑hour gig is 4.2. And 38 of last month’s deploys look like the same change shipped twice — once from the command line and once by the GitHub build that the same push triggered. That is about 570 credits of pure duplication, more than half a month’s grant, spent shipping nothing new.

Fix that first. It is worth more than every optimisation in the next section combined, and it takes an afternoon: pick one deploy path, turn the other off.

SCALE

What happens if it does go viral

Traffic is the honest thing to project, because it is the only line that grows with users. Deploys do not; a thousand artists need the same number of releases as one.

That last row is the answer to the fear. Serving 200,000 gigs a month is a rounding error against $100,000 of revenue. For scale: Stripe’s own cut of each $10 subscription — 2.9% plus 30¢, which is 5.9% — is very slightly larger than the entire server bill. Payment processing costs more than the servers, and nobody thinks of that as a threat to the business.

The ratio is fixed, and that is the important structural fact. Cost per gig is constant; revenue per artist is constant; artists play a roughly constant number of gigs. There is no point on the curve where traffic overtakes the subscription — unlike, say, an AI feature, where cost per user rises with how much they use it.

LEVERS

Every way to make it cheaper, ranked

Ordered by what they actually save, not by how interesting they are. The first two need no code.

  1. 01

    Stop double‑deploying

    Turn off either the command‑line deploy or the GitHub build, so one push ships once. Nothing about the product changes.

    ≈570 credits/month · 6.8× all your gig traffic
  2. 02

    Batch releases

    104 production deploys in a month is roughly four a day. Shipping twice a day instead of four times halves the largest line on the bill and changes nothing a user sees.

    ≈750 credits/month
  3. 03

    Split the poll payload

    Every poll returns the whole song list, which changes a handful of times a night. Send a version number and let the phone skip the list when it has not moved — fewer bytes, and the storage reads behind them can be skipped too.

    −60% bytes, −40% compute
  4. 04

    A three‑second edge cache on the poll

    Twenty phones in one room ask the same question within the same second. Caching the answer for three seconds at the CDN turns twenty function calls into one, and three seconds is inside the fastest rung — nobody could tell.

    up to −80% compute in a full room
  5. 05

    Fewer than fifteen reads

    The poll reads fifteen documents. Several — the setlist, the songs to learn, the feedback state — change once a night, not once every three seconds. Read them only when the show’s timestamp has moved.

    −4 reads/poll ≈ −25% compute
  6. 06

    Keep the show in memory

    The real fix, and the largest. One object per live show, holding the state in memory, answering polls without reading storage at all. This is what Cloudflare’s Durable Objects are for and it is the only change that makes the reads structurally disappear rather than fewer. It is also a rewrite of the hot path, so it is a decision for a thousand artists, not for now.

    $0.028 → $0.002 per gig · 13×
ALTERNATIVES

Would a cheaper host help?

This is the part worth reading twice, because the intuitive answer is wrong.

Moving hosts would make it worse

Cloudflare bills nothing for bandwidth and very little per request, which is why everyone assumes it is cheaper. But it charges $0.50 per million key reads, and MySet does fifteen reads on every poll — 90,450 reads in a single gig. Itemised, those reads cost more than Netlify charges for the whole invocation.

The platform is not the cost driver. Fifteen reads per poll is. Fix that and every platform is cheap; do not fix it and no platform is. The only reason Durable Objects wins is that it deletes the reads rather than repricing them.

Two more that were considered and are not worth it. Running your own server — a $12/month box would serve all of this and more, but it introduces a machine to patch, a database to back up, and a thing that can be down at 10pm on a Saturday; the saving is real and the cost is your weekends. Websockets instead of polling would cut requests to near zero, but neither Netlify nor a serverless function can hold a connection open for three hours, so it means the same always‑on server, with the same trade.

THE ONE RISK

Video is the line that can move

Everything above holds because MySet moves tiny amounts of data. Clips on community posts break that assumption, and they are the only feature that can.

A 3 MB clip watched a hundred thousand times is 300 GB — about 6,000 credits, roughly $40 — from one post. That is more than a month of everything else. Three things keep it bounded, and all three are already in the code: clips are re‑encoded on the phone and capped at 3 MB by the server; every clip has a poster frame so the feed loads a still and the video only downloads when somebody actually taps play; and each clip is cached at the edge for a year.

Worth a note in the calendar: if clips get popular, bandwidth moves from 8% of the bill to most of it, and that is the moment to put them behind object storage with free egress rather than serve them from a function.

VERDICT

What to do, in order

Now. Stop the double deploys and move to Netlify Pro. You are at 2,475 credits against a 1,000‑credit grant, and $20 for 3,000 is cheaper than $9 plus top‑ups. That is a today decision regardless of anything else here.

Before beta. Levers 3, 4 and 5 — the payload split, the three‑second edge cache and the read trimming. Together they take a gig from 2.8¢ to about 1.5¢ and, more usefully, they halve how long the poll takes on bar wifi.

At a thousand artists. Revisit lever 6. Not before: it is a rewrite of the one path that must never break, and at $560 a month it is not yet buying anything worth the risk.

Never, on this evidence. Move hosts to save money. The numbers say it would cost more.

The short version. The business model is safe. Serving the audience costs about 5% of what the audience’s artist pays you, at every scale that matters, and the structure of the app is why. The thing quietly costing you money right now is not the app at all — it is deploying it four times a day.

How to check any of this. Run python3 tools/loadsim.py in the MySet repo to reproduce every per‑gig figure, with different room sizes and set lengths; ./credit-burn.sh pulls the live deploy count from Netlify; node --import ./test/register.mjs test/cost.mjs prints the reads per poll and fails if they grow. The payload size came from curl against production on 5 September 2026, the 155 ms from a timing measurement inside a live function, and the credit rates from Netlify’s and Cloudflare’s published pricing on the same day. The polling behaviour is the only modelled input; the model is the ladder in public/vote.html, and it is stated in the simulator’s header so it can be argued with.
