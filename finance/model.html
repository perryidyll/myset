Does installing the page as an app change the server bill?

No, not today. The voting page asks the server the same way whether it is open in Safari or installed on the home screen: it walks the same 3 s → 10 s → 25 s ladder, it goes silent the moment the screen is off in both cases, and the app’s service worker never caches the live show — every ask still reaches a function. Installing changes nothing the server is charged for.

What would change the bill is replacing asking with telling — push instead of polling. That only works for an installed app on iPhone, it needs a permission prompt, and it fits the artist (who installs the Studio anyway) far better than a stranger in a bar. So the honest reasons to reward installing are retention, not cost: a home-screen icon brings people back, it opens faster, and it makes a future “your artist plays tonight” notification possible.

Three things worth knowing before nudging anyone to install: the voting page never actually asks (the install card lives on the city page, not the page the room is holding); on iPhone an installed app keeps its own storage, so a fan who installs mid-gig becomes a stranger and loses paid votes — ask after the show, never during; and installing cannot be checked by the server, so never attach money to it. Sound reasons to ask anyway: Safari wipes a site’s memory after seven days of not visiting, an installed app keeps it, and that memory is the fan’s credits and requests. The dial “Installed as an app” stays on the page so this is not forgotten; it deliberately moves no cost line.

If a big artist shows up: the tidal wave

First, the honest part: at 10,000 phones today’s code does not get expensive, it stops working. Every poll reads every fan record in the room, so a poll gets heavier as the crowd grows (the “extra poll time per person” dial), and in a big room every vote wakes every phone, so the ladder never backs off. A 300-person concert is fine (about 12 polls a second). An arena is hundreds of function calls running at once and thousands of document reads a second against the same handful of keys, plus a vote stampede every time a song ends. The “On this host” column in the size table says which is which — for today’s Netlify setup, and for each other host in the list. The 50,000 festival is not a bill; it is an outage. No real night has had more than 8 voters (short demos aside), so every line above that is simulation.

Ranked by what they protect against, cheapest first. The first three need almost no code.

  1. Auto-recharge is already on. Add a burn watchdog. At zero credits Netlify pauses every site on the account (iohm.io and idyllenterprises.com too). Auto top-ups turn that outage into a bill, but Netlify has no spending cap and its alerts only watch the monthly allotment. A five-minute check of credits during a live show that flips the brake below is the only real ceiling. Half a day of scripting.
  2. Sell the arena instead of absorbing it. A booked, prepaid “Arena / Festival” tier for anything above about 500 expected phones, priced off these numbers. A big artist calling becomes a sales event, and the booking is what switches on the big-room mode. Policy in an hour, the form in a day or two.
  3. Terms that say so. Above 500 attendees needs an Arena booking; MySet may slow the refresh, pause voting or cap participants to protect the service. Two hours with a template.
  4. Let the server set the pace (the brake). One field in the poll answer — “ask again in N seconds” — lets a huge room be slowed to 15 s while the bar set stays at 3 s. Switch it on in the dials to see what it does to the size table. A day.
  5. A kill switch. One flag that puts a room into read-only (votes paused, tally every 60 s) without a deploy. It has to stop the reads, not just the writes. Half a day.
  6. Cache the answer for three seconds. A room of any size becomes one function call every 3 s; only requests and bandwidth remain per phone. The arena drops to about $9, the festival to about $530 — and the concurrency problem disappears. Needs the head-count write moved off the poll first. Two to three days. Pick “Netlify + 3-second cache” in the host list to see it.
  7. Big-room mode and a phone cap. Above N phones the poll sends a tiny “what changed” answer; above a hard cap without a booking, late arrivals watch the screen. Two to four days, after the two above.
  8. Edge rate limits per phone. Bounds a script or a bot, not a real crowd (a whole bar shares one address on venue wifi), so set generously. An hour.
  9. Votes that queue instead of failing. In a stampede a losing write retries for up to 16 seconds and then says “busy”. Fewer tries under load, a “vote queued” answer, and eventually one small document per fan. A day for the short version.
  10. The rewrite, when a booking pays for it. One object per live show held in memory: the reads disappear rather than get cheaper. Two to four weeks, and honest pricing includes the per-second duration charge — the earlier “13× cheaper” figure left it out; with it the gap is about 1.4× at bar size and larger at arena size.

Do the first three this week: they cost almost nothing and remove the only failure that is truly catastrophic. Then the brake and the kill switch, then the cache. Everything else waits for a real booking.

What is measured, what is published, what is guessed

  • measured Bytes per poll (2,530, from production), reads per call (15 per poll, 4 per vote, 21 per Studio poll — counted through the real code on 5 Sep 2026), 42 ms per document read. The 155 ms per poll was a probe before a fix; the account’s own bill implies 70–110 ms, so 120 is the default here.
  • simulated The 20-phone, 3-hour gig (about 5,900 polls, 4.8 credits on this page; the repo’s simulator says 6,030 and 4.2 because it runs at 155 ms with no Studio and no extra poll per action) is the polling ladder run against a model of how people glance at phones — reproducible, but no real night has had more than 8 voters (short demos aside). Every crowd figure above that is simulation from measured inputs.
  • published Netlify credit rates and plan prices; Cloudflare, Vercel rates; Stripe’s card, Billing, Connect and dispute fees — all read from the providers’ own pages on 5 Sep 2026 (links in the footer).
  • guess How many artists, on which plans, playing how often to how many people; what a room spends; how many gigs get promoted; fixed costs. These are the dials. Turn on “Use real shows” when the numbers exist.
  • Whose Stripe fee is whose. Tips and vote packs are charged on the artist’s connected account, so Stripe’s card fee on them is the artist’s cost, not MySet’s; MySet’s cut arrives with no extra Stripe fee on it. Subscriptions and featured shows are charged on MySet’s own account (a US account, checked 5 Sep 2026 — so US rates, with about one card in eight international so far — measured on the account’s own charges, so artists’ cards may run higher), so their card fee, the 0.7% Billing fee, disputes and refunds are MySet’s. Express account and payout fees are charged to the platform — and the code pays artists out daily, so a busy artist can cost 25¢ a night in payout fees; the dial defaults to 8 payouts a month and a weekly schedule would cut it. Uncollectable negative balances on artists’ accounts also land on MySet, hence the small bad-debt line.
  • Two things the code promises that the money does not yet do. The referral “free month” only moves a date in the registry; a paying referrer keeps being billed. And the founder’s own gigs cost server credits but produce no platform revenue. Neither is modelled as a separate line.
  • The free tier’s four-gig cap is applied: a free artist who wants six nights gets four. The refused nights are shown as a line in the formula.
